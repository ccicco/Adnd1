The Dragon

# The Return of Conan Maol

Paul Karlsson Johnstone

There are a few sayings which have in them the skirt of the bag-pipes, calling men on to be something more than ordinary men. One of them is credited to a grim, red-headed Irishman who lived — if he lived at all, for there are those who say he is only a fiction—about 1650 years ago. But the words credited to him are still alive. Listen . . .

“A man lives after his life—but not after his honor.” Those are the words of Goll (the One-eyed) Mac Morna, a hero of Connacht. He lived and died up to those words. But the story is that he had a brother who was unlike him — Conan Maol, Bald Conan, who was fat and lazy and bitter-tongued. We can easily believe that last — “The Irish are a fair people” wrote Samuel Johnson, “they never speak well of themselves.” But was he really so fat and lazy and cowardly as the stories tell? Let us remember that Goll and Conan were men of Connacht, and most of the Fenians tales we have were shaped in Leinster or Munster — and that to get an Irishman really fighting mad, you need to slander his mother — not his father — or his county — not Ireland as a whole.

One tale goes that once on a day the Fenians, all of them, faced a host of pirates at a place which since that day has been known as Cnoc-an-Air, the Hill of Slaughter — near Galway — and a champion-fighter came forth from the pirates. And the Fianna pushed out Conan Maol to meet him. And Conan shook from poll to feet, so that both hosts laughed to see him — Yes, but, look you, he went out, with sword and shield.

“Why are you shaking so?” grinned the pirate champion. “Is it afraid you are?”

“You would be shaking too” said Conan quickly. “If you could see who walks a step behind you!”

The pirate champion craned his neck to peer behind him — and Conan’s blade lifted the head from his shoulders. So the Fianna, exulting in Conan’s victory, came charging and made mincemeat of the pirates. Is that the story of a coward, or the story of a hero, not unflinching like his brother, but still able to win an omen-duel, fighting between two hosts? Just an old story? Maybe, maybe . . .

In April of 1784 a young Irishman with the splendid name of Theophilus O’Flannagan took a walk to the mountain of Slieve Calainn in County Clare. He had received a letter from an antiquary, Charles Vallancey, concerning an early Gaelic poem, which told of an inscribed stone on that hill. Taking a friend with him, young Flannagan went in search of the stone. He found a likely-looking stone but — it was totally uninscribed! Theophilus might have gone home and written to Vallancey that the story was untrue. Luckily, he was more persistent. A cottager told him there was another stone on the other side of the mountain, with marks on it, seemingly meaningless. Flannagan hunted it, and eventually found it — a stone rather over seven feet long and three wide, placed on “a kind of tumulus (burial mound).” And there was an inscription, of a sort!

There were no letters of the Latin sort we are accustomed to — just a straight horizontal line with notches above and below and across it. Vallancey was able to recognize, from Flannagan’s sketch, that here were letters in the very old Irish writing called Ogam — but what did they say? He knew of a Gaelic poem, part of which was translated by O’Keamey as follows:

The ferocious warrior, Conan  
Was not at Gabra in the mighty strife  
For on Beltane of the previous year  
At an assembly convened to worship the sun  
The bold hero was slain treacherously  
By the Fianna of Finn.  
On the dark mountain of Callan  
His grave was made on the northwest slope.

Sadly plaintive was his dirge

And his name in Ogam is on the dressed stone.

Those were famous names. Conan of course was Conan the Bald, son of Morna and brother of Goll, sometimes friends and sometimes enemies of the still more famous Finn. Gabra (pronounced Gowra) is a place near Tara, the scene of the famous battle between the High King Cairbre and Oscar, grandson of Finn, in which both leaders were killed. This was said to have been fought in A.D. 283, which would make Conan’s death take place in 282 — but Irish chronology at this period is not very firmly based, so it is safer to say that these things happened around A. D. 300.

Beltane, the “Fire of Bel” is our May Day. Bel is one of the names of the Celtic sun god. That the pagan Irish worshipped the sun we know from St. Patrick’s Confession. And as to the statement that Conan was killed at “an assembly convened to worship the sun,” we have the fact that Mount Calainn was also known as Altair no Greinne “Altar of the Sun,” and that the hilltop is crowned by a stone “cromlech” called by that name. Everything fitted neatly. But what did the inscription actually say?

Theophilus Flannagan, aided by Charles Vallancey, aided by a Gaelic manuscript owned by Vallancey, made out the inscription to read

FAN LI DAF ICA CONAN COLGAC COS OBMDA

So there was Conan’s name! It was all perfect — too perfect, said the self-appointed experts of that day. In the first place, said a historian named Ledwich, in such a wild unsheltered spot such an inscription could not have endured for 1500 years. This was shown to be completely untrue. O’Flannagan was charged with forging both the Gaelic poem and the Ogam inscription. The poem was genuine, and the Ogam inscription was genuine. But — what did it say?

One word, COLGAC was clear. It means “swordsman.” It was the name of the Caledonian chief who fought against Agricola at the Craubian Mount in A.D. 83. There it was the name of a man. Here it seems to be the title of Conan — perhaps in memory of his duel at Cnoc an Air.

Eventually the experts (so called) accepted the inscription — all but the name of Conan, which they emended to COSAF — a name totally unknown. But were the experts right? Only two letters are disputed. The first is four or more probably five downstrokes. It can be read as either S or N. So it is as plausible to read CONA — as it is to read COSA. The last letter is the crux. It is given as three downstrokes. This should mean F, or, more correctly at that date, V. But three “intercepts” — strokes which start above the line but go on below it — mean GN — and Gaelic Conan derives from Continental Celtic *Cunagnos* “Doggie.” This was no name of contempt, but a proud title, and anyone who has seen an Irish Wolfhound will instantly understand why. So, the central words of the inscription should be read as CONAGN COLGAC — “Conan the Swordsman.”

It needs to be understood that by no means every Gael of that time could decipher Ogams. It was a knowledge reserved for kings and poets and Druids. Thus the man who worked with hammer and chisel on the stone probably did not understand what he was putting down. Some poet or Druid told him what to write and paid him — paid him well, one hopes, for the toil of perhaps a week’s hard work with hammer and chisel, far from his friends and family, on a bare and windswept hilltop, under weeping Irish skies. I hope his pay was not less than a good cow, then the standard currency of Erin.

The Druid or poet will of course have carefully told the stonecutter exactly what marks to put on the stone which he had roughly hewn out. And the men of that time, who had usually no convenient writing materials, had very good memories. And he had the best of motives for remembering. He may well have been paid in advance, but the friend of Conan who had paid for the epitaph might return at any time, and the penalties for enraging a poet or Druid were heavy indeed. He might simply blow a *dúi fulla*, a “fluttering straw” in the face of the man who had offended him, and that worthy would become a *geilt* a “wild man,” naked and running across country in great leaps, impossible for any sane man even to imitate, sleeping if at all only in the boughs of a tree, able at times to talk as sanely as ever to his friends and kin, but forever barred from his former life . . .
